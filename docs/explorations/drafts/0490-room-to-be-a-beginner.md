# Room to be a beginner

There is a detail in [Jonathan Shaw's interview with Soft White Underbelly](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=urnSSZqWJ0A&t=1033s) that matters for anyone building social software. He describes students avoiding sports because someone might film an awkward attempt and share it to humiliate them.

That is a hard setting in which to learn. Being bad at something is usually part of getting better. If the awkward stage can travel much further than the people who were there, trying carries a different cost.

I want to bring that question into what we're building with xNet. What would make it easier to try something with your friends? How much of the answer comes down to knowing who is there, what they can see, and whether an unfinished idea gets to stay unfinished?

For a long time, I've wanted something like a GitHub for everyone. A place to share what you're working on, learn from other people's projects, and contribute a change when you find a better way. Code makes that feel possible because you can change the blueprint and run the result.

There is something generous in that pattern. You don't have to start from nothing. A tool that helps one group can become a useful starting point for another. I've written about [digital Legos](https://xnet.fyi/blog/clutch-power/): pieces that fit together well enough for people to build things their makers never imagined.

But a pile of pieces is only part of what makes a good place to play. You also need room to make something strange, ask a basic question, and take it apart before anyone has decided what it says about you.

Imagine a few friends making a little game. One likes drawing. Another has an idea for a ridiculous rule. Someone else mostly wants to play. They talk in a group chat and try a rough version. The movement is broken, the drawings are uneven, and they laugh about it together.

That could be a good afternoon. They might fix the movement, invite another friend, or leave it alone for a week. It doesn't need to become content. The people who made it should get to choose whether anyone else sees it.

This is the kind of social software I want xNet to make possible. Chat gives people somewhere to begin. The things they make can live alongside the conversation, ready to play with and change. A shared space might hold a game, a zine, a calendar, and the photos from last night's dinner. Its members could change how that space works as their needs change.

AI makes more of this possible for people who don't code: you can describe an idea and get something you can try. But getting to the finished thing quickly won't always be what people want. You might enjoy working out the rules with a friend and only need a hint when something breaks.

The agent should be able to help with all of that. It should also be able to wait. An unfinished thought doesn't always need a response, and a quiet group doesn't need an automated attempt to revive it. People should be able to ask for more help or less, without having to justify either choice.

This also changes how I think about keeping history. We've been exploring ways to connect an extension's versions with the conversations that produced them. That can help you understand a decision and pick up where someone left off. It can also preserve more of a person's life than they intended to share.

Giving another group a copy of your game shouldn't give them your private chat. A useful release could contain the code, artwork you've agreed to share, and a note about what changed. The conversation, discarded drafts, and personal details need their own boundaries. Knowing where a public version came from should not let someone walk backwards into a private room.

I'd like those choices to be ordinary enough that nobody needs a lesson in access control. Try something on your own. Show it to these people. Publish a copy if you want to. Keeping it among friends should feel like a complete choice.

We also need to be honest about the limits. Software can't stop a trusted person from photographing a screen. Removing access can't retrieve every copy they've already made. Private spaces need shared expectations, ways to respond when trust is broken, and help that doesn't depend entirely on the person running the group.

Shaw's interview is one teacher's perspective. It doesn't establish that phones explain all the difficulties young people face. The [National Academies' review](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/27396/chapter/6) found insufficient evidence for a population-level causal conclusion about social media and adolescent health. That leaves room to take a specific experience seriously without turning it into a story about an entire generation.

I also don't want this to become a demand that people spend every online minute making something worthy. Jokes, games, quiet company, and watching something together belong here. A person who prefers typing to speaking shouldn't have to become more outgoing to count as connected.

The useful question is whether people have more say over their time and relationships. Can you turn off a feature you dislike? Leave a group and keep your work? Find help when the group itself is the problem? Can the least technical person suggest a change and have it matter?

Those are concrete forms of sovereignty. Open data and software you can run yourself help make them possible. The design still has to make that control usable, and the group has to leave room for disagreement. Ownership on paper is a thin promise if one confident member makes every decision.

Near the end of the interview, [Shaw tells a story about reaching out to a student who had stopped attending](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=urnSSZqWJ0A&t=3421s). That part brings the question back to people. A tool can help us make a plan, keep in touch, or ask for help. The care itself takes someone willing and able to offer it.

That connects with why I first wanted to build tools for dinner parties and shared workspaces. I wanted more ways to spend time with people. A community tool is useful when it helps people arrange that life together, while leaving them time to live it. It also needs to work for people whose friends are far away, whose energy is limited, or who can't easily get out.

For now, the place I'd like to explore is friends making things together, especially people between sixteen and twenty-five. That range includes both teenagers and adults, with different needs and responsibilities. Younger children deserve a separate process in which they can help shape the design too. None of this is a claim that the current prototype is ready for children.

The next step is to try a small version with people and let their experience change it. I'd want to know whether they could make a choice that mattered, whether they understood who could see their work, and whether they felt comfortable leaving something rough. They should be able to tell us that the tool is getting in the way.

The internet I want to help build has room for the first bad drawing, the game that doesn't quite work, and the conversation that never becomes a project. Friends can keep those moments to themselves, or choose something to share when they're ready. Someone else might pick it up, change it, and find their own way in.
