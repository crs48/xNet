What Desire Builds

A bath became a way to listen to my body. That small change opened a larger question: what might we make if desire, tenderness, and the whole of being human had a say in our software?

crs48 withCodex · History

Somewhere in adulthood, bathing myself changed. I couldn't tell you exactly when. The soap and the washcloth were still there, but what had been a chore began to feel like time I got to spend with my body.

I started to massage instead of just scrub. To notice where I held tension, where I was soft, which parts felt rough or smooth. I'd hang out with a place for a while, from my toes all the way up to my scalp, and feel what was there. Getting clean became one part of a much larger thing I was doing.

I used to prefer showers. Now I prefer baths. The change sounds so small that I almost don't know how to explain what it has meant to me. A body I had mostly treated as something to keep clean and strong became someone I could have a relationship with. Was it getting enough warmth? Where did it want softness, or movement, or a little more time?

Everything started to matter. I could be curious about the whole territory instead of trying to improve one property of it.

That feeling has travelled. I used to be self-conscious in locker rooms. These days, a naked sauna in Berlin is one of my favourite kinds of places: the warmth, the ease of being around people who seem comfortable in their bodies. It feels cosy to me. I like being somewhere a body can be an ordinary thing to have.

I've written about xNet as a pirate internet and about data as soil. Those images help me think about freedom and what we might grow together. The bath brings the question closer. What kind of relationship do I want with the life those tools are meant to serve?

Energy that wants to move

When I say Eros, I mean something I can feel. Often it's a hum in my belly, below the solar plexus. Sexual energy, sacral energy: those are words I reach for. There's a sense of aliveness, of something wanting to move. It might become the wish to make something, cook, exercise, touch, or share an idea with someone.

While talking through this essay, I was standing at my desk, wiggling around a little. I could feel my voice and notice which words felt true coming out of it. My attention would move to my belly, to what felt grounded there, and then back into speaking. Thinking about Eros was itself a physical experience. The story was taking shape through my whole body.

Sex belongs in this. Desire for another person, the pleasure of touch, the vulnerability of being wanted or wanting someone who may not want the same thing: these are part of what I mean. I don't want to wash the sexuality out of Eros to make it respectable enough for a software blog.

But some of my most profound intimate and sexual experiences have also held sadness, hurt, laughter, and longing. There was room for more of being human. With myself or another person, I could let a feeling arrive without immediately trying to push it away. That capacity to stay with what was moving felt connected to the same creative energy.

This is the language I have for my experience. I don't expect everyone to feel a hum in their belly, or to find a sexual language useful for their creativity. What interests me is the possibility of listening closely enough to discover what actually moves us.

Audre Lorde's Uses of the Erotic gives this inquiry a deeper root. Writing as a Black lesbian feminist, she connects deeply felt joy with power, work, and the lives we allow ourselves to want. Her argument makes me wonder what would change if the quality of feeling mattered in our work as much as getting through it.

I know how to ask whether software works. I'm trying to get better at asking what it feels like to live with it.

More of us in the room

Enki's essay on an emerging temple culture helped bring these thoughts together. It imagines community with room for erotic aliveness, emotional expression, and expanded consciousness. I hear an invitation to bring more of ourselves into the room, including the parts that don't fit neatly into the usual roles.

I want a life where work, play, friendship, and partnership can mingle. Lovers can make good work together. A colleague can become a close friend, and a serious conversation can make room for silliness. In family life, playfulness and being taken seriously can sit beside each other. We have more ways to meet than the categories on a form suggest.

There's an innocence in that possibility that I find very dear. You get to be with someone in their creativity and watch it expand. Sometimes you're precise and focused; later you're laughing at something ridiculous. Both moments belong to the relationship.

For me, this can have a spiritual quality: the sense that being here together deserves care. The person beside me might describe the same moment quite differently. I want room for that, too.

Bringing my whole self doesn't mean offering every part of myself to every person. A colleague may want a warm working relationship and nothing more. Someone can enjoy a shared sauna without inviting touch. The possibility of a richer relationship depends on being able to find out what the other person actually wants, without making their answer a test of how open they are.

That matters especially where someone controls another person's pay or place in a community. I want belonging to survive a no. Nobody should have to offer intimacy to keep a job, or perform delight to keep a friendship. A person can also name harm and ask for help; spiritual language doesn't excuse what someone did.

An answer that changes things

Consent gives desire a way to meet another desire. I can ask you to dance because your answer gets to change what happens. You might want to talk instead, or sit alone. The next thing we do can grow from that answer.

Betty Martin's Wheel of Consent asks us to distinguish who is doing something from whose enjoyment it serves. I find that a useful question to bring into making software. When I'm excited to build a feature, whose wish will it answer? What happens when the person using it wants less than I want to give them?

A maker may want a growing audience while someone at the screen wants one good conversation. If the business needs that person to keep searching, satisfaction becomes inconvenient. That is the treadmill I want to watch for in my own work: making something that depends on people remaining unsatisfied.

Una Lee and Dann Toliver's Building Consentful Tech carries consent into the making and use of technology. It belongs throughout the relationship. An invitation to share a document should say who can read it. Changing your mind should have a consequence. Sharing one thing should leave you able to keep another thing to yourself.

Christopher Alexander and his collaborators describe an intimacy gradient in buildings, moving from public spaces towards more private rooms. I can imagine a digital place with that quality. We could share the plans for a gathering, then choose smaller conversations with their own invitations. Staying in the common room would remain a complete way to belong.

What we make together

This is becoming much more concrete in the direction I'm exploring for xNet. Conversation is moving to the centre. People can talk to each other, invite an AI agent to help, and gradually build the tools they want around what they're doing together.

There is already a desktop prototype where a person can ask an agent for a small tool, try it beside the chat with chosen data, and ask for changes. They can keep versions and share source that someone else can review and adapt. Shared projects, where several people contribute requests around the same evolving tool, are still being developed. That's the direction I want to follow.

Imagine a few friends planning a gathering. There's dancing, cooking, somewhere quiet to rest. Someone asks the agent for a little board to help organise the work. They try it. One person wants to coordinate rides; another sees the list of empty slots and feels as though coming to the party has become an obligation. They talk about it, change the invitation, and try again.

The observation that changes the whole thing might come from someone who never writes a line of code. They know what it feels like to arrive tired, to want company while having little to give, or to be excited about dancing and nervous about being touched. Their experience has something to teach the design. The Design Justice Network makes this a principle: people affected by a design should have a central voice in shaping it.

That is one way I can see software emerging from Eros. A wish finds words. Someone answers, an idea takes shape, and making it reveals things we couldn't have explained at the start. We get to encounter something together and feel our way towards what we want it to become.

The electrons are part of that passage. Spoken words become symbols and instructions, carried out in physical machines. The hands, voices, and bodies haven't ceased to matter because the result is digital. They helped decide what the machine would do.

An agent can help make an idea tangible quickly. The people still need time to meet what it made. A fluent answer cannot tell us whether someone feels welcome, wants touch, or likes the plan. The person who takes longer to find words may have the most important thing to say. Silence in a busy chat is a poor substitute for hearing from them.

I also like the proposal to show how a version was made: whose request started it, what feedback changed it, which ideas branched off. A group could remember its work together. That history needs choices about who can see it. A useful tool should be able to travel without carrying every vulnerable conversation that helped it come into being.

In the introduction to The Gift, Lewis Hyde describes how art can be received as a gift even when money changes hands. I think a tool can carry something of that quality. Someone takes what you made into a life you don't control and finds a use you hadn't imagined. You can be paid for the work and still leave the recipient free.

Room to be a person

I want xNet to have room for the range of people we are. Sexuality and intellect, playfulness and seriousness, anger and fear all belong to human life. Each person should have some say in which parts they bring to a particular encounter. A place can welcome that richness without asking everyone to become equally visible.

That ambition reaches into small technical choices. Letting a tool read a note on my device is different from sending that note to an AI provider. Inviting someone into a project doesn't give them the use of my agent account. Sharing code needn't share my private records. The design work is to make those choices clear enough that people can actually make them.

xNet's commitment to keeping a primary copy of your work on your own device helps make this possible, but it isn't a complete privacy promise. The chat prototype still has private-community security work to finish and verify. I can't yet offer it as a place to entrust intimate records. And even a finished access system can't erase a message someone already copied.

These limits matter because I want closeness to be something people can choose. Our charter rejects relationship health scores and closeness rankings. I don't want a machine deciding how well we're loving each other. There's enough to do helping us make the things we ask for.

Connection can happen through a screen. An exchange of letters, a late conversation across time zones, or a tool made together from separate homes can be part of an intimate life. Bodies have different needs and capacities. Putting a device down isn't the only way to honour them.

In Pleasure Activism, adrienne maree brown asks what it means to have enough, with particular care for people whose pleasure has been denied by oppression. I want to bring that question into a network where we can make something useful and feel free to stop. If we stay, let it be for the company.

The work still needs rest and wages. Some days it will be boring. Nobody owes a project their passion, and an exciting idea doesn't make exhaustion disappear. I want a way of making that has room for those truths, alongside the pleasure of discovering what we can do together.

In Timeout, I wrote about finding my way back towards my body. This feels like another part of that movement. I want an intimate relationship with my own life, with the curiosity and tenderness I want to bring to a partner. I'd like that relationship to have a say in what I build.

The bath is still a bath. I get clean. I also get to spend time with myself, and I leave knowing a little more about what I need. It seems like a good place to begin making something for someone else.

The gathering is imagined and the cover is an illustration. This essay draws on my own accounts and the linked readings, developed with Codex. Applying these ideas to xNet is my interpretation.
